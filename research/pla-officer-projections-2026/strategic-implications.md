# Strategic Implications: what the purge does to Chinese military power

Companion to [`README.md`](README.md). The main report asks *who* will lead the PLA. This file asks *so what* — what the leadership crisis does to readiness, to the 2027 milestone, to Taiwan risk, to the wartime chain of command, and to the succession question that dominates the 2036–2041 horizon.

Positions are attributed to named analysts, because on most of these questions the serious literature genuinely disagrees, and the disagreement is more informative than any synthesis.

---

## 1. Readiness: what actually degraded

### 1.1 The measurable indicators

- **Decision latency quadrupled.** CSIS's China Power Project measures the interval from political directive to deployment in response to Taiwan behaviour: **3–4 days in 2024 → 19 days before *Strait Thunder-2025A* (April 2025) → 12 days before *Justice Mission-2025* (December 2025)**.
- **The nomenclature downgrade.** *Strait Thunder-2025A* was described in PRC sources not as an **演习 (exercise)** but as an **演练 (drill)** — CSIS characterises it as "discrete training activities with minimal connection," lacking the usual complexity. This is a Chinese-source admission of reduced ambition and the single most citable qualitative indicator in the open literature.
- **The fill state of the operational chain.** Of 52 key operational positions tracked by CSIS: **21% filled, 44% interim or acting, 23% outright vacant.** Nearly half the operational chain is run by caretakers — a finding this report independently reproduced from the theater and service rosters (README §2.3).
- **Scale.** CSIS counts **101 senior officers** removed or missing since 2022 (36 officially purged, 65 missing/likely), **~52% of the PLA's 176 designated leadership billets**, and **87% of three-star officers affected** (41 of 47 serving in 2022 or promoted since). Fravel counts 38 theater-command leaders dismissed and 56 deputies, cutting the replacement pool by roughly a third.

### 1.2 The finding that matters most: it is a *divergence*, not a decline

Chinese military activity did not simply fall. In 2025, around Taiwan:

- PLA aircraft ADIZ incursions **rose 22.4%** to 3,764; PLAN vessel presence rose 42% against baseline; carrier days beyond the first island chain rose from 32 (2024) to **58** (2025); South China Sea operations hit a record 163.
- But **China–Russia joint exercises collapsed from 14 (2024) to 6 (2025)** — the lowest since 2020, "with multi-domain exercises absent."

**Unilateral sortie generation — which a squadron or a flotilla can do on its own — went up. Multi-actor, staff-intensive, diplomatically coordinated activity went down.** That divergence localises the damage precisely: what broke is the **joint, cross-theater, staff-planning layer** — exactly the layer the purge removed. Suyash Desai supplies the control case: despite six senior officers purged from the Western Theater and the Xinjiang and Tibet military districts, exercise counts on the India front "remained largely constant." Routine single-theater ground training is unaffected.

A second control comes from an unexpected direction: Ken Allen's bottom-up compilation of PLA Army training reporting — which does not discuss the purges at all — finds junior-level problems (poor combined-arms coordination, conscription cycles leaving replacements 3–4 months late) that long predate 2023. Not everything wrong with the PLA is the purge's fault.

### 1.3 Documented capability shortfalls attributed to corruption

The US Department of Defense's 2025 report states plainly:

> "Corruption in defense procurement has contributed to observed instances of capability shortfalls, such as **malfunctioning lids installed on missile silos** or possibly the **pier-side sinking of the PLAN's first Zhou-class [Type 041] submarine** as it prepared for sea trials."

It adds that CCDI investigations of vice-ministerial-and-above officials rose by almost a third in 2024, "with much of that increase coming from the defense sector," and that **at least 26 senior managers of state-owned arms suppliers have been investigated or removed.** The 2024 edition records that in July 2023 the PLA opened a review of weapons procurement **back to 2017**, and that in March 2024 He Weidong told an NPC delegation the CMC would crack down on **"fake combat capabilities" (假战斗力)** — the regime's own phrase for the problem.

**The counter-argument, from the same source.** DoD's 2024 report assesses that the Rocket Force investigation "likely resulted in the PLARF repairing the silos, **which would have increased the overall operational readiness of its silo-based force**." Any claim that the purge degrades capability has to engage the possibility that, in at least one documented case, it fixed something.

## 2. The 2027 milestone: what it is, and a shift worth noticing

**2027 is not an invasion date.** It is the centennial of the PLA's founding and a capability benchmark, introduced publicly in October 2020 (internally set in late 2019), sitting inside a three-step plan running to 2035 and 2049. Andrew Erickson calls it "a waypoint to a waypoint." ASPI's Attrill and Wilford call it "a capability target, not an invasion deadline."

The goal has three named components, rarely reported:
- **战略决胜 "strategic decisive victory"** — credibly prevailing in a conflict at acceptable cost, tracked against a Taiwan contingency with US involvement
- **战略制衡 "strategic counterbalance"** — deterrence, including nuclear, sufficient to restrain US involvement
- **战略慑控 "strategic deterrence and control"** — capacity to limit horizontal escalation and deter opportunism by other states

And it is explicitly **conditioned on national economic development** — Xi's formula requires modernisation "in step with the modernisation of the country," which gives the leadership latitude to recalibrate. That conditionality matters for any fifteen-year projection.

**A finding in its own right: DoD's own framing moved between editions.** The 2024 report calls 2027 "a **modification, not a compression** in timeline." The 2025 report says: "China expects to be able to **fight and win a war on Taiwan by the end of 2027**." One year apart, pulling in opposite rhetorical directions. Neither says an invasion is scheduled — but an analyst citing only one edition will misstate the official US position. Cite both.

### Does the purge push 2027 back?

| Position | Advocates | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| **Slipping** | Fravel, Wuthnow, Lin (all CSIS, Feb 2026) | Empty billets prevent planning and training; promotion requires 3–5 years per grade, so rebuilding takes time; Xi may "push those deadlines back by a few years" |
| **Not slipping** | DoD CMPR 2025; Erickson; Keary (ASPI) | "The PLA continues to make steady progress toward its 2027 goals"; the purges are "a speedbump, not a showstopper"; 2025 saw significant gains in amphibious lift and strike |

**Note the asymmetry rather than picking a winner: the "slipping" camp reasons from personnel inputs (vacancies, promotion timelines); the "not slipping" camp reasons from capability outputs (hulls, missiles, sorties). They are measuring different things and can both be right.**

A third position deserves more attention than it gets. **John Culver** — a former senior US intelligence officer on East Asia — argues the driver is neither Taiwan nor the United States but fear of "a potential repeat of Tiananmen-era internal turmoil," with Xi seeking "political reliability above military effectiveness for internal stability scenarios." On this reading the entire external framing is misapplied. Culver notes the 1989–92 purges replaced six of seven military region commanders; the current wave is "more intense at the service chief and deputy department levels."

## 3. Taiwan risk: the structured disagreement

### Camp A — degraded force, lower near-term risk

**Bonny Lin** (CSIS): "In the near term, given the significant vacancies, it would be incredibly difficult for China to launch large military campaigns against Taiwan." **Fravel**: the operations most affected are precisely those requiring "significant national mobilization and integrated joint operations." **Attrill and Wilford** (ASPI) give four mechanisms — reduced capability confidence, organisational friction, command risk-aversion, and the fact that 2027 is a capability target — under a deliberately hedged headline: *"Xi's military purge probably cuts imminent Taiwan war risk. Probably."* **Ying Yu Lin** predicts "tactical activism combined with strategic caution."

### Camp B — consolidated control, higher later risk

**Joel Wuthnow**'s three-phase model is the most useful timeline available, and it is a bridge rather than a pure position:

- **Near term (1–2 years): risk down.** "If war broke out tomorrow, there would be no high command to lead it."
- **Medium term (late 2020s – early 2030s): the peak.** New commanders will be more technically competent but structurally unable to object: "**Whoever he appoints to fill important vacancies will be in a diminished status and less likely to push back.**" His analogy is the one to sit with: "New generals might be in a weaker position than those who came before them to push back if Xi entertains the same war optimism that convinced Putin to attack Georgia and Ukraine."
- **Long term (post-Xi): risk down.** A successor inherits military-autonomy problems without Xi's personal authority.

**Joe Keary** (ASPI) puts the loyalty-selection problem bluntly: officers will be "selected primarily for obedience rather than professional judgement," and on Zhang Youxia — "no pedigree, no combat record and no past loyalty confer immunity when control is at stake." **Brian Hart** adds a specific loss: **Zhang Youxia and Liu Zhenli were the last two senior officers with actual combat experience** (the 1979 Vietnam war), and both are gone.

### The sharpest single finding

**Thomas Christensen**: the purge "may have little or no impact on such military options short of invasion," and — the sentence that should trouble anyone reading the "purge lowers risk" conclusion too comfortably — **"A blockade of Taiwan would be relatively easy for the PRC, and there is little that Taiwan or the United States could do militarily to stop it."**

**The purge degrades the hardest option and leaves the easiest coercive option intact.** Set that against the 2026 pattern — fighter sorties at a three-year low in the first half of 2026 while mainland government-vessel sightings around Taiwan rose 83% month-on-month and 120% year-on-year, and China Coast Guard vessels made first-ever entries into restricted waters at Pratas and Itu Aba — and the purge looks less like a brake on coercion than a **redirection** of it toward the gray zone. (The 2026 tempo data is single-sourced; treat as provisional.)

### The theory that structures the debate

**Jun Sudduth** identifies two opposing effects running simultaneously: purges of senior officers **weaken elite ability to constrain the leader** (one finding: military purges within the past three years reduce the likelihood of a successful removal attempt by nearly 13%), while also **degrading military competence**, making victory less certain. Saddam Hussein and Putin are the cases. Xi is coded as a personalist dictator with significant ambition, whose judgements will be "influenced by filtered information" from loyalist officers. Sudduth's conclusion — that strategy "cannot afford to privilege one mechanism over the other" — is the right frame for this report.

### A negative finding worth stating

**No analyst in the serious open-source literature argues the diversionary-war case** — that Xi would launch or escalate a Taiwan conflict *in order to* resolve or distract from the purge. The nearest things are Sudduth's permissive-condition argument and Attrill/Wilford's "coercive proof-of-life" (Beijing may need visible activity to demonstrate the force still works — framed as sub-war coercion). Anyone advancing the diversionary reading carries the burden of proof.

## 4. Command and control: how would they actually fight?

**What is missing.** Bonny Lin states it most precisely: "**Within the Central Military Commission, there is no operational-track leader left.** Vice Chairman Zhang Shengmin is a political commissar and is ill equipped to oversee joint and cross-theater operations." There is no Joint Staff Department director; no Political Work Department head; no Joint Logistics Support Force commander; and all four service commanderships have been held in acting capacity.

**What is not missing.** Two structural facts, both easy to get wrong:

1. **Xi personally holds the title of commander-in-chief of the CMC Joint Operations Command Center**, announced in 2016. The apex wartime node is not vacant — it is occupied by Xi himself. What is missing is the professional staff layer between him and the theaters.
2. **The Defense Minister is not in the chain of command.** Dong Jun leads international military engagement. Anyone modelling a wartime chain through the Ministry of National Defense is modelling it wrong.

**Theater command survives.** DoD's reporting on *Joint Sword-2024A* and *-2024B* records that the **Eastern Theater Command's own Joint Operations Command Center led both exercises** — including the first integration of China Coast Guard vessels, which DoD expects to become the norm. The designed wartime chain runs Xi → CMC JOCC → theater JOCC → forces, with the Joint Staff Department providing planning at the CMC level.

**Assessment.** Single-theater operations are executable; the broken layer is precisely the cross-theater, joint and mobilization layer — which maps exactly onto the observed 2025 activity data and onto Fravel's judgement. **Logistics is the least-discussed vulnerability:** the Joint Logistics Support Force has no commander, and while a blockade is sustainable on theater assets, an invasion is not sustainable without joint logistics. That asymmetry reinforces Christensen's conclusion independently.

**The serious counter-case.** K. Tristan Tang (Jamestown) argues the CMC's July 2025 political-work regulations are a systemic overhaul targeting "inappropriate interpersonal relationships" rather than money, and that Xi is **deliberately** slow-rolling senior appointments — including the Political Work Department directorship — until new evaluation systems prove out. On this reading the vacancies are **policy, not damage**, and expanded operational roles for political cadres create "command redundancy through stronger layered leadership and distributed resilience." This is the only structured argument that the current arrangement is designed rather than broken, and it deserves to be taken seriously.

## 5. Succession: the mechanic that governs the 2036 and 2041 horizons

This is the part of the literature that most directly reshapes this report's long-horizon projections.

### 5.1 The CMC chairmanship transfers last

Nan Li's treatment (written in 2021, before the purge, which makes it a clean baseline) establishes the precedent:

- **Deng Xiaoping** held the CMC chairmanship **1982–1989**, past his other offices.
- **Jiang Zemin** was general secretary 1989–2002 but CMC chairman **1989–2004** — two years past the party post.

**"CMC chairmanship historically extends beyond the party general secretaryship."** Li's baseline path: a designated successor becomes **CMC vice-chairman at one Congress and takes the top job at the next**, with the incumbent retaining the chairmanship for a transition period so the successor can gain governing experience.

Joseph Torigian supplies the theoretical reason it works this way: authoritarian succession outcomes are determined by the military and security apparatus, and leaders like Khrushchev and Deng won by "making promises to gain the support of the military and security services." Whoever controls the CMC controls the succession — which is exactly why it goes last.

### 5.2 The arithmetic this forces

If the precedent holds:

- A general-secretary handover at the **22nd Congress (~2032)** implies CMC transfer around **2034–2036**.
- A handover at the **23rd Congress (~2037)** implies CMC transfer around **2039–2041**.

**Both dates land inside this report's projection window**, which is why §5.3 of the main report treats 2041 as scenario-dependent rather than name-dependent.

### 5.3 The single best leading indicator

Jonathan Czin's observation is the operational one: **no civilian has sat on the CMC since 2012**, and Xi has dismantled the Deng-era succession machinery — abolishing presidential term limits, breaking the two-term norm, and turning the vice-presidency into "a professional cul-de-sac."

Combined with Nan Li's template, this yields a concrete, checkable test:

> **If no civilian is named to the CMC at the 21st Party Congress in autumn 2027, a general-secretary handover in 2032 becomes structurally difficult, and 2037 becomes the earlier realistic date. A civilian CMC appointment in 2027 is the strongest available signal that a 2032 transition is being prepared.**

This is added to the falsifiable register in [`forecasting-model.md`](forecasting-model.md).

### 5.4 The risk the literature under-argues

Nan Li's relative optimism rested on the PLA being factionally too weak to act as kingmaker. The purge cuts both ways against that: it has further weakened PLA institutional autonomy (supporting Li), while making loyalty **personal to Xi rather than institutional** (supporting Torigian's concern).

**A successor inherits a PLA conditioned to loyalty to Xi personally, with no institutional habit of loyalty to the office.** That is the sharpest under-argued risk in the entire fifteen-year horizon. Wuthnow's Phase 3 — post-Xi risk falls, because a successor lacks Xi's authority — assumes an orderly handover; a contested one inverts the conclusion entirely.

### 5.5 The demographic footprint arrives inside the window

Wuthnow and Czin/Matthias both expect the replacement generation to be younger, more technically literate, and — having "grown up in a rising China" — more confident. Officers commissioned into the post-2027 rebuild reach theater command in the **late 2030s**: precisely the 2036/2041 window. And Czin flags a nearer pivot that belongs even in a long-horizon report: **Taiwan's 2028 presidential election**, with Xi approaching 80 and the CMC still being reconstituted, is the first real stress test of the rebuilt command.

On who fills the CMC in 2027, Czin and Matthias reach a striking conclusion: only **three** plausible non-purged officers are promotable — **Yang Zhibin, Han Shengyan, and Zhang Jichun** — and **none holds Central Committee membership**, so Xi "would likely need to reach into the deputy theater command or corps level." They call what is coming **"helicopter promotions"** eighteen months before the Congress. That is an independent corroboration of this report's §5.0.

## 6. An unmet research need

There is **no published wargame or structured scenario exercise that models PLA leadership under purge stress.** The Michaels–Williams wargame (Syracuse, August 2025, 25 experts playing from Beijing's perspective) is the only one that treats command dysfunction as a variable at all — and its participants, noting a command structure "riddled with political interference," pushed the Beijing team toward precision strikes and political coercion **rather than amphibious invasion**, and found attacking US forces made little strategic sense from Beijing's viewpoint.

The widely cited CSIS 2023 invasion wargame (24 iterations) is a materiel-and-geometry model: **it does not model PLA command quality or leadership failure as an independent variable.** Anyone citing it as evidence about the *post-purge* PLA is over-reading it.

The inputs for a proper leadership-stress wargame now exist — quantified decision latency (3–4 days → 12–19 days), quantified vacancy rates (44% acting, 23% vacant), and a documented record of how acting commanders have performed. Building one is a legitimate research gap to flag.

## 7. Ten things to carry forward

1. The tempo story is a **divergence, not a decline**: unilateral sorties up 22.4%; China–Russia joint exercises down 57%. The joint/staff layer broke, not the force.
2. **演习 → 演练** — the PRC's own nomenclature downgrade is the best qualitative indicator available.
3. **DoD's framing of 2027 shifted between the 2024 and 2025 reports.** Cite both editions or misstate the official position.
4. The 2027 goal has three named components and is **explicitly conditioned on economic development** — both cut against the invasion-date reading.
5. **Christensen's asymmetry**: the purge degrades invasion capability and leaves blockade capability intact. The 2026 data show substitution toward coast-guard coercion.
6. **Wuthnow's three-phase model** (near-term down, late-2020s/early-2030s peak, post-Xi down), with **Sudduth** as the general theory, lets the debate be presented as structured rather than merely contested.
7. **No analyst makes the diversionary-war argument.** Say so.
8. **Xi personally holds the CMC JOCC command-in-chief title; the Defense Minister is not in the chain of command.** The apex is occupied; the staff layer beneath it is not.
9. **The CMC chairmanship goes last** (Deng +3 years, Jiang +2). The leading indicator for 2036/2041 is whether a **civilian is seated on the CMC in autumn 2027**.
10. **The purge's demographic footprint lands inside the projection window**, and the generation it produces is expected to be more confident and less able to say no.
