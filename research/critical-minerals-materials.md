# Critical Minerals & Advanced Materials: The Substrate of the Whole Atlas

*Part of the [China Defense-Industrial Atlas](https://claude.ai/code/artifact/ecc69e75-bcc3-46cf-a303-3b95f8ac9b4c). Compiled from open sources through August 2026.*

Every other essay in this atlas is, at bottom, a materials essay. A fighter engine is a superalloy problem before it is a design problem. A radar is a gallium-nitride problem. A guided-weapon fin actuator, a drone motor, a satellite reaction wheel — all of them turn on a rare-earth magnet. The platforms get the parades and the model numbers; the materials get almost no coverage at all, which is exactly why they are the most underrated layer of Chinese military-industrial power. This essay maps that layer — and it is the one place in the atlas where the strategic story runs decisively *China's* way.

The materials layer splits cleanly into two halves that point in opposite directions. In **critical minerals and magnets**, China is not a supplier the world can pressure — it is the chokepoint the world runs through, and since 2023 it has learned to squeeze. In **advanced structural materials** — the superalloys, titanium, carbon fiber and special steels inside platforms — China spent two decades climbing from dependence to rough independence, and is now closing the last gaps at the very top. One half is a weapon; the other is a vulnerability being retired. Together they are the substrate the rest of the atlas sits on.

## Part I — The mineral chokepoint, and the weapon built on it

### The magnet stack, top to bottom

Start with the single most important material in modern weaponry that almost nobody names: the **sintered neodymium-iron-boron (NdFeB) permanent magnet**. It is what makes an electric motor small and strong — which means it is inside precision-guided-munition fin actuators, radar and sonar transducers, drone and missile servo motors, satellite reaction wheels, submarine propulsion motors, and the F-35's own systems (each airframe uses on the order of 900 pounds of rare-earth material). There is no bulk substitute that does not cost performance. And China makes roughly **90% of the world's supply** and controls **over 90% of global processing capacity**.

That dominance is a vertical stack, and the [PLA Supplier Atlas](https://claude.ai/code/artifact/9ba26611-80ca-456b-b489-06bce1b350ed) now maps all three of its layers:

- **The ore and the separation, upstream.** [China Northern Rare Earth](https://claude.ai/code/artifact/9ba26611-80ca-456b-b489-06bce1b350ed), the Baotou giant, separates and refines the ore from **Bayan Obo** — the largest rare-earth deposit on Earth — into the neodymium and praseodymium oxides everything downstream needs. It holds roughly 50–60% of China's domestic light-rare-earth quota. The quota itself is the lever: Beijing sets it, and it decides who separates what.
- **The magnets, midstream.** **JL MAG Rare-Earth** (Ganzhou, ~40,000-ton 2025 capacity) is the world's number-one maker of *high-performance* sintered NdFeB, and **Zhongke Sanhuan** (spun from the Chinese Academy of Sciences) is the other pillar and a leader in dysprosium-lean formulations that stretch scarce heavy rare earths. Between them they anchor the finished-magnet layer the entire world's motors depend on.
- **The heavy rare earths that make magnets survive heat.** Dysprosium and terbium are what let an NdFeB magnet hold its magnetism at the temperatures inside a motor or a munition. China's control of *these* — the medium and heavy rare earths — is even tighter than its control of the light ones, and it is where the 2025 export weapon was aimed.

### From receiving controls to imposing them

For a decade this map ran one way: the US and its allies controlled the chokepoints (lithography, jet engines, chip-design software) and China absorbed the pressure. Since 2023, in the mineral layer, China has run the same playbook in reverse — and the timeline is a tit-for-tat ladder:

- **August 1, 2023** — China imposes export licensing on **gallium and germanium**, the compound-semiconductor and infrared substrates. China produces roughly **80% of the world's refined gallium and 60% of its refined germanium**; export volumes to some destinations collapsed by more than 95% within months. This came weeks after tightened US chip-tool controls. The message was legible.
- **August 2024** — the licensing regime expands to **antimony** (hardener for munitions and a flame-retardant input; China is the largest producer), plus graphite and certain superhard materials.
- **December 3, 2024** — a day after a fresh US chip-equipment and Entity-List action, China moves from licensing to an outright **ban on exports of gallium, germanium, antimony and superhard materials to the United States**. The reciprocity was explicit.
- **February 2025** — controls extend to **tungsten, tellurium, bismuth, molybdenum and indium** — tungsten being the material behind armor-piercing penetrators and machine-tool cutting bits.
- **April 4, 2025** — the big one: licensing on **seven medium and heavy rare earths** (terbium, dysprosium, samarium, gadolinium, lutetium, scandium, yttrium) **and on the magnets themselves**. Processing can take up to 45 working days, and licenses are issued buyer-by-buyer — turning paperwork into a metering valve. This is the action that rattled automakers, aerospace primes and defense contractors worldwide, because it hit finished magnets, not just raw oxides.
- **December 1, 2025** — the escalation that shows the ambition: controls reach **"internationally made" products that contain Chinese-sourced rare earths or are made using Chinese processing technology** — an extraterritorial reach deliberately modeled on the US Foreign Direct Product Rule that had been used against Huawei. China is not just controlling exports; it is claiming jurisdiction over foreign supply chains built on its inputs.

The pattern is unmistakable: this is **weaponized interdependence** (Farrell and Newman's term), and China is now wielding the "chokepoint effect" it spent a decade only receiving. See the [Chokepoint & Leverage Ledger](https://claude.ai/code/artifact/9b07ec41-f9ff-4863-a0cf-c0fd43b9b11a) for how this leverage nets against China's remaining dependencies.

### The catch: leverage you use is leverage you spend

Two caveats keep this from being a clean Chinese win, and both matter for an honest read.

First, **the licensing weapon is reversible and was reversed.** The rare-earth controls became the centerpiece of the 2025 trade truce: in exchange for the US pausing its own escalations (including the EDA-software cutoff), Beijing eased the magnet regime and, later in 2025, signaled a softer stance and paused parts of it. That is the tell — the controls are a *bargaining* instrument, calibrated to be traded, not a permanent embargo. Notably, China's 2025 rare-earth exports still hit their highest level since at least 2014 even with the regime in place, because the point was leverage and revenue, not autarky.

Second, **using the weapon accelerates the escape from it.** Every time Beijing meters magnets, it hands the US, EU, Japan and Australia the political will and the price signal to build non-Chinese supply — MP Materials in the US, Lynas in Australia and Malaysia, and a wave of allied processing investment. The substitution horizon is long (five to ten years to stand up separation and magnet-making at scale, and heavy rare earths are the hardest part), which is why the leverage is real today. But it is a depreciating asset, and Beijing knows it — which is part of why the controls are calibrated as a valve rather than a wall.

## Part II — The structural-materials layer: a dependency being retired

The other half of the materials story is the quiet two-decade project that gets even less attention: the alloys, fibers and steels inside the platforms. Here China was genuinely dependent as recently as the 2010s, and here it has mostly — not entirely — closed the gap.

- **Single-crystal superalloys and turbine blades** — the hot-section materials that decide how hot a jet engine can run, and the classic reason Chinese engines lagged. [AECC BIAM](https://claude.ai/code/artifact/9ba26611-80ca-456b-b489-06bce1b350ed) (the Beijing Institute of Aeronautical Materials, AVIC's old 621) developed the **DD6 second-generation single-crystal alloy**, which its own chief designer claims matches or beats comparable Western second-generation alloys at lower cost, and [Gaona Aero Material](https://claude.ai/code/artifact/9ba26611-80ca-456b-b489-06bce1b350ed) supplies the powder-metallurgy superalloys for turbine disks. The WS-15's production ramp (see the [aero-engine essay](./aero-engine-catchup.md)) is, underneath, a story about these two closing the metallurgy gap. This is the tier where a *residual* dependency remains — the highest-temperature, most exotic single-crystal and thermal-barrier-coating work is where Western labs still lead — but the trajectory is unambiguous.
- **Aerospace titanium** — [Western Superconducting](https://claude.ai/code/artifact/9ba26611-80ca-456b-b489-06bce1b350ed) (Xi'an) qualifies titanium alloys into engines, airframes and submarine hulls, and appears in nearly every advanced platform's bill of materials. China is now a net *exporter* of aerospace-grade titanium — a full inversion from twenty years ago.
- **Military carbon fiber** — for years [Guangwei Composites](https://claude.ai/code/artifact/9ba26611-80ca-456b-b489-06bce1b350ed) (Weihai) was the only military-qualified Chinese source, closing out a hard dependence on Japan's Toray; the J-20 and the CH-series drones fly on its tows. It is now joined by Zhongfu Shenying and others — a solved problem where it used to be a chokepoint.
- **Special and bearing steels** — [Fushun Special Steel](https://claude.ai/code/artifact/9ba26611-80ca-456b-b489-06bce1b350ed) supplies the high-temperature and bearing steels inside engines and airframes: chronically troubled as a business, persistently indispensable as a supplier — the recurring pattern of this whole layer.
- **Additive manufacturing** — [Bright Laser Technologies](https://claude.ai/code/artifact/9ba26611-80ca-456b-b489-06bce1b350ed) (Xi'an) prints large titanium airframe and engine structures for AVIC, AECC and COMAC. Metal 3D printing is becoming a signature Chinese shortcut around traditional forging bottlenecks — a case where China is not catching up but arguably leading in industrial application.

The through-line of Part II is the mirror image of Part I. Where minerals are leverage China holds *over* others, structural materials were a dependency China held *itself*, and has spent two decades engineering away. What remains is a thin band at the very top — the most exotic single-crystal alloys, the highest-end coatings — narrow enough to name and narrowing every year.

## The bottom line

The materials layer is where the atlas's central paradox is most concentrated. This is simultaneously the domain of China's **greatest strategic leverage** — a near-monopoly on the magnets and minerals the entire Western defense-industrial base runs on, now actively weaponized through a licensing regime built to be turned like a valve — and the domain of one of its **quietest success stories**, the two-decade climb out of dependence on foreign superalloys, titanium and carbon fiber.

For an analyst, three things follow. First, the mineral leverage is real but *depreciating*: it is maximal now and erodes as allied supply comes online over five to ten years, and every use of it funds the escape. Second, the structural-materials dependency is nearly retired, so betting on a materials chokepoint to constrain Chinese platforms is betting on a door that is closing. Third, and most important, the layer everyone ignores is the layer that decides the others — which is precisely why it belongs at the bottom of the atlas, holding everything else up.
