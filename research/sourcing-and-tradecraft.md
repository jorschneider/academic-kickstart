# Sourcing & Tradecraft: Where to Actually Look

*Part of the [China Defense-Industrial Atlas](https://claude.ai/code/artifact/ecc69e75-bcc3-46cf-a303-3b95f8ac9b4c). Compiled from open sources through August 2026.*

Most English-language writing about China's defense industry runs on the same dozen sources: the annual Pentagon report, three or four think-tank studies, a handful of trade publications, and each other. The result is a literature that is broadly correct and strangely uniform — everyone citing the same estimates, and the estimates hardening into facts through repetition rather than verification.

The [methodology page](./methodology-and-confidence.md) grades how much to trust this atlas's ratings. This page is the other half: **where the evidence actually comes from, and how to go get it yourself.** It documents the primary channels — most of them Chinese-language, most of them free, nearly all of them underused — and it opens with three findings that no amount of reading Western secondary sources will give you.

---

## Three things only the primary sources tell you

### 1. Beijing publishes its own chokepoint map

The original brief behind this atlas was to find the *unheralded* suppliers — the small firms that matter more than their profile suggests. It turns out the Chinese state publishes a list of them.

The **"Little Giants" programme** (专精特新"小巨人"企业, *zhuanjing texin xiaojuren*) is a MIIT designation for small and medium enterprises that, in the ministry's own framing, have mastered technologies in key industrial links **to fill supply-chain gaps**. It is a tiered system: local "innovative SMEs," then provincial "specialized and sophisticated SMEs" (专精特新中小企业), then the national-level Little Giants. The 2018 criteria were concrete — annual revenue between ¥100–400 million (roughly $14–56m), annual profit growth of at least 10%, R&D staff above 15% of headcount, and at least five innovation patents.

Two things make this analytically valuable rather than merely interesting.

First, **the programme was explicitly built around chokepoints.** It was shaped by China's 卡脖子 ("stuck neck") technology anxieties after the 2018 US–China rupture; MIIT's initial goal was 600 Little Giants between 2018 and 2020. By December 2024, six batches had been announced covering roughly **14,600 firms**, overshooting the 14th Five-Year Plan target of 10,000 by 2025. Read structurally, the roster is *Beijing's own assessment of where its supply chains are weakest* — a vulnerability map published by the party-state, sector by sector. Where designations cluster, the state believes it is exposed.

Second, it inverts the usual open-source problem. Western analysts normally have to *infer* which obscure component makers matter. Here the government has done the ranking and published the names. Several firms already in the [PLA Supplier Atlas](https://claude.ai/code/artifact/9ba26611-80ca-456b-b489-06bce1b350ed) — Western Superconducting among them — carry exactly this pedigree. The list is, in effect, a state-curated target deck for anyone mapping the supplier base.

### 2. There is no "transfer mechanism" because the officials are the same people

"Military-civil fusion" is usually discussed as a *process* — technology moving from a civilian sector into a military one through some channel. The institutional reality is blunter, and it is visible in personnel appointments.

**Shan Zhongde** was appointed a Vice Minister of MIIT — the ministry that runs the Little Giants programme and civilian industrial policy — in February 2024. In January 2025 he additionally became **Administrator of the China National Space Administration, Director of the China Atomic Energy Authority, and Director of SASTIND**, the State Administration for Science, Technology and Industry for National Defense. One official, simultaneously: civilian industrial policy, the civil space programme, the atomic energy authority, and the administration of defense science and industry. (He is a Tsinghua-trained mechanical engineer, a Chinese Academy of Engineering academician since 2019, and formerly president of Nanjing University of Aeronautics and Astronautics — one of the "Seven Sons of National Defense" universities.)

The pattern repeats below the centre. [Jamestown's reporting on robotics subsidies](https://jamestown.org/policy-support-for-robotics-firms-shows-defense-integration/) found that the Beijing municipal bureau administering civilian robotics support is headed by an official who *concurrently* directs the Beijing Municipal Defense Science and Technology Industry Office. Jamestown's conclusion is the right one: **no additional transfer mechanism is required.** A civilian firm receiving industrial-policy support is already inside the institutional perimeter of defense procurement, because the same people administer both.

This reframes one of the atlas's four throughlines. The Pentagon's 1260H list keeps widening — 188 entities by June 2026, now including Alibaba, BYD and WuXi AppTec — and the usual reading is American overreach, sweeping in commercial firms on a thin dual-use pretext. The personnel evidence suggests a less comfortable interpretation: the category is unbounded because **the Chinese institutional architecture genuinely does not separate the two at the top.** Whether that justifies any particular listing is a policy question. But it is not a definitional accident.

The concrete case is instructive. **Unitree Robotics** — the humanoid and quadruped maker on the 1260H list, which has objected to the designation — holds the Little Giants designation and received roughly **¥76 million (~$11m) in tax incentives in the first nine months of 2025 alone**, plus about ¥32 million in direct grants between 2022 and September 2025. Whatever Unitree's own intentions, it is a designated national champion inside a support architecture run partly by defense-industrial officials.

### 3. The PLA publishes its shopping list twice a year

Since January 2015 the PLA has run an official weapons-and-equipment procurement portal, **weain.mil.cn**, established under what was then the General Armament Department and now sits with the CMC's Equipment Development Department. It carries procurement notices, requirement announcements, policy documents and supplier registration.

The operationally useful detail is the **cadence**: procurement information is published on the **first working day of every January and July**. That is a date an analyst can put in a calendar. Access is tiered — visitor, registered user, certified user — so the open tier shows less than a registered Chinese defense contractor sees, but the open tier is not empty. Journalists have broken real stories from it: SCMP derived reporting on a prospective **airborne laser weapon** programme directly from procurement notices on the site.

The portal exists because the PLA wanted to widen its supplier pool and cut procurement barriers — a transparency created for industrial-policy reasons that happens to be legible to outsiders. That is the general shape of the best Chinese sources: they are open because opacity would defeat their domestic purpose.

## The channel map

Seven families of sources, with what each is good for and where each fails. Tiers refer to the evidentiary weighting in the [methodology page](./methodology-and-confidence.md).

### Chinese state and primary documents *(Tier 1)*
- **weain.mil.cn** — PLA procurement notices; January/July cadence; tiered access.
- **MIIT Little Giants batch lists** — named SME rosters by batch and province; the published vulnerability map above.
- **SASTIND / CNSA / CAEA** — defense science-and-industry administration; personnel appointments are themselves data, as above.
- **NPC budget releases (each March)** — the official defense budget line; the growth rate against nominal GDP is a [tripwire](./indicators-and-warnings.md).
- **Five-Year Plan texts and MIIT implementation documents** — priority naming (the 14th FYP named CJ-1000 engine certification explicitly).
- **GJB military standards** — the national military standard series; standards reveal required performance envelopes.
- **PLA Daily (解放军报) and CCTV-7** — the venue where the PLA talks to itself, and usually the first confirmation that a system exists *in inventory* rather than in development.
- *Limits:* aspirational language, political framing, and silence on anything genuinely sensitive.

### Corporate disclosure *(Tier 1–2)*
Chinese defense primes are unlisted, but many **subsidiaries are publicly traded** and file real financials — the single most underused channel in the whole field.
- **AVIC Shenyang Aircraft (600760.SS)**, **AVIC Xi'an Aircraft (000768.SZ)**, **AECC Aviation Power**, **Inner Mongolia First Machinery**, **North Navigation**, **JL MAG**, **Western Superconducting**, **Zhongke Sanhuan**.
- What they yield: revenue and backlog, capacity expansion capex, related-party transactions with the parent (which trace intra-conglomerate work), inventory swings that precede delivery surges, and occasionally segment disclosure that implies output rates.
- **Corporate and institute official accounts (企业/院所公众号)** — milestone posts, facility photographs and, most usefully, **recruitment notices**: a job posting names the technical area, the platform, and sometimes the programme an institute is staffing for. Posts vanish without notice once a topic turns sensitive, so archive on sight.
- *Limits:* military revenue is often aggregated or redacted; related-party disclosure is inconsistent; a listed subsidiary sees only its slice.

### Chinese technical literature *(Tier 2–3)*
- **CNKI / CJFD** — the China National Knowledge Infrastructure covers essentially all mainland academic journals (8,000+ titles, 1915–present); **CQVIP** and **Wanfang** are alternatives; **East View** resells Western access.
- **CNIPA patent filings** — assignee-based searching on institutes and firms shows where R&D is actually going, often years before publicity.
- **Dissertations and conference proceedings** — graduate work at the "Seven Sons" defense universities is frequently the most detailed public description of a subsystem.
- *Limits:* publication lags; sensitive work is classified out of the literature entirely; a paper proves research interest, not fielded capability. This is where the temptation to over-read is strongest.

### Translation and doctrine *(Tier 2)*
- **CASI (China Aerospace Studies Institute, Air University)** — the *In Their Own Words* series publishes full translations of PLA doctrinal texts: the 2013 and 2020 *Science of Military Strategy*, *Science of Campaigns*, *Lectures on the Science of Space Operations*, PLA terminology guides — plus organizational studies of AVIC and the services. Free.
- **CMSI (China Maritime Studies Institute, US Naval War College)** — China Maritime Reports, CMSI Notes, "Red Book" monographs, and **CMSI Translations** of Chinese military maritime writing. Free via USNWC Digital Commons.
- **Andrew Erickson's bibliographies** maintain access guides to both.
- *Limits:* doctrine describes intent and concept, not fielded capability; translations lag publication.

### Geospatial and physical observation *(Tier 1)*
- **Commercial imagery** (Planet, Maxar) as analysed by FAS, CSIS and the OSINT community; **Sentinel** for free multispectral.
- **H.I. Sutton** on undersea and naval, **@RupprechtDeino** on aviation — individual analysts who reliably beat institutions to airframe and hull identifications.
- **AIS and ADS-B** for shipping and aircraft movement.
- *Limits:* imagery shows presence, not performance; cloud cover and revisit rates create gaps; the analysis is only as good as the analyst's identification.

### Trade, customs and financial flows *(Tier 1–2)*
- **China customs data** and **UN Comtrade** — the flows that reveal what an opaque industrial base is buying and selling; energetics and precursor exports leave trails here.
- **USGS and IEA** commodity data for minerals and processing shares.
- **BIS Entity List and DoD 1260H filings**; **OFAC actions** — each listing carries findings that are themselves evidence.
- **C4ADS** and similar for corporate-network and evasion mapping.
- *Limits:* mis-declaration, transshipment through Hong Kong and third countries, and deliberate obfuscation.

### Western specialist analysis *(Tier 2)*
Used properly, as a pointer to primary material rather than a substitute for it.
- **CSET (Georgetown)** — particularly Hannas and Chang on China's science-and-technology intelligence apparatus.
- **MERICS** — the *Accelerator State* report on the Little Giants system.
- **Jamestown China Brief** — consistently good on institutional and personnel questions, as above.
- **SemiAnalysis** and **TechInsights** on compute and physical teardowns.
- *Limits:* the uniformity problem this page opens with.

## Five tradecraft rules

1. **Prefer the physical to the declared.** A teardown, a satellite image, a customs record and a shipped tonne are harder to spin than a capacity announcement or a press release. Where the atlas states a threshold in [tripwires](./indicators-and-warnings.md), it is stated as a physical quantity for this reason.
2. **Personnel appointments are data.** Who holds which post — and how many posts at once — often reveals institutional intent more reliably than any policy document. The Shan Zhongde case above took ten minutes to verify and reframes an entire throughline.
3. **Read what the state says to itself, not what it says to you.** Five-Year Plan implementation documents, procurement notices and standards are written for domestic execution. White papers and spokespeople are written for you.
4. **A published paper is research interest, not capability.** The most common failure mode in technical-literature analysis is treating a CNKI hit as evidence of a fielded system. It is evidence that someone was funded to think about it.
5. **Note when a source's absence is the finding.** No public figure exists for Chinese munitions output; that void is why the [ground-systems](./ground-systems.md) comparison is epistemically lopsided, and saying so is more useful than importing a number from a secondary source that invented one.

## What this changes about the atlas

Three concrete revisions follow from the material above, and they are folded into the corpus rather than left here:

- **The MCF throughline is stronger and more specific than "dual-use is fuzzy."** It rests on shared administration and dual-hatted officials — a structural claim that is checkable against appointment records.
- **The unheralded-supplier layer has a state-published index.** The Little Giants roster is the highest-yield starting point for anyone extending the supplier map, and future additions to it should start there rather than from Western coverage.
- **Several atlas claims have a named, dated place to go verify them.** The NPC budget each March, weain.mil.cn each January and July, the DoD CMPR each December, MP Materials' quarterlies, TechInsights teardowns. An atlas that names its check dates is one that can be maintained rather than merely written.
